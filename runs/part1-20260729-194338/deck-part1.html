Rope Lay: Direction of Twist

The lay refers to the manner in which wires are helically laid into strands, and strands into the rope.

Lang’s LayRegular Lay (Ordinary Lay)
Wires in the strand are twisted in the SAME direction as the strands. Wires and strands twisted in OPPOSITE directions.
Result: Difficult to handle, less resistant to crushing/distortion, tends to untwist. Safety Advantages:
  1. More structural stability
  2. More resistance to crushing and distortion
  3. Less tendency to rotate under load
  4. Less possibility of kinking
  5. Easy in handling during installation

Selection Guideline 2: Flexibility

Flexibility is critical where sheaves are small or where the rope makes many bends. It is achieved by using a large number of small-diameter wires.

6 × 7 Construction6 × 19 or 6 × 37 Construction
Few wires of relatively large size. Too stiff for hoisting purposes. Suitable only as haulage and guy ropes. Large number of small-diameter wires. Highly flexible. The standard choice for hoisting applications.

Selection Guideline 3: Abrasion Resistance

Where the wire rope is likely to drag through gritty material or across a stationary object, abrasion resistance becomes the governing design factor.

The 6 × 7 Advantage

Because the 6 × 7 construction consists of a few, very large-diameter outer wires, it inherently provides better wear resistance against harsh, gritty surfaces compared to flexible multi-wire ropes.

The Stress Problem in Wire Ropes

The analysis of stresses in a wire rope is complicated because individual wires are subjected to two concurrent forces:

  1. Direct tensile stress from the suspended load.
  2. Bending stresses as the rope wraps around the sheave.
This differential length change creates additional severe tensile bending stresses in the outer wires.

Direct Tensile Stress from Load acts downward at both ends of the wrap.

Bending Stress Derivation: Step 1

From pure bending mechanics

The bending stress in a single individual wire is governed by the standard flexure formula:

σb = Mb·y / I

Where the distance from the neutral axis (y) is half the individual wire diameter (dw):

y = dw / 2

Substituting y into the flexure formula:

σb = Mb·dw / 2I   (Eq. a)

Bending Stress Derivation: Step 2

Incorporating sheave geometry

The elastic curve equation relates bending moment to the radius of curvature (r):

Mb / EI = 1 / r

Because the rope wraps around the sheave, the radius of curvature (r) is equal to the radius of the sheave (D/2):

r = D / 2

Substituting this into the elastic curve equation yields:

Mb / EI = 2 / D   (Eq. b)

Bending Stress Derivation: Equations 23.1 and 23.2

Equating expressions (a) and (b) yields the bending stress for a straight solid wire:

σb = E·dw / D   (Eq. 23.1)

The Corkscrew Correction:

The individual wires of the wire rope make a corkscrew figure in space — a path different from that of the straight circular wire bending around the sheave. We must replace the standard modulus (E) with the effective modulus of elasticity of the rope (Er).

σb = Er·dw / D   (Eq. 23.2)

Equivalent Bending Load (Equation 23.3)

What is Equivalent Bending Load (Pb)?
It is a hypothetical tensile load that would induce the exact same stress as the bending stress. Converting bending stress into a hypothetical tensile load allows designers to simply add it to the static loads.

Pb = σb·A   (where A is the metallic cross-sectional area of the rope)
Pb = A·Er·dw / D   (Eq. 23.3)

Dynamic Failure Modes: Fatigue & Wear

While static loads dictate initial sizing, wire ropes ultimately fail dynamically while passing around sheaves.

Fatigue Failure

Caused by the continuous bending and straightening of the rope as it passes over the sheave. This results in severe fluctuating stresses that crack individual wires over time.

Wear Failure

As the rope bends, individual wires physically slide against each other and against the sheave groove. This results in the gradual wearing away of load-carrying metallic material.

The severity of both failure modes is directly tied to the pressure between the rope and the sheave.

Sheave Pressure Derivation (Equation 23.4)

(a) cross-section   (b) rope over sheave, D = sheave diameter

Force per unit length of the wire rope is (p · dr).

Considering equilibrium of forces in the vertical direction:

2P = p · dr · D
p = 2P / (dr·D)   (Eq. 23.4)

Where:
P = tension in the rope (N)
dr = nominal diameter of wire rope (mm)
